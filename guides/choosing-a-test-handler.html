Choosing a Test Handler for Your Package

Turret, strip, gravity feed or KGD sorter. The way your device arrives at test decides most of the answer. Here is how we narrow it down.

By John Eliel C. Baladjay, Business Operations Manager · September 25, 2026 · 4 min read

When a plant tells me "we need a handler", my first question is never about the machine. It is about the device: how big is it, and how does it arrive at test? Tubes, trays, reels, strips, or wafer. That one answer rules out most of the options before we ever talk about brands or budgets.

This guide covers how we narrow it down with engineers, and where people get caught.

Start with the format, not the machine

A handler has one job: present each device to the tester, make reliable contact, and sort the result. How it does that depends on what it is handed. Four formats cover most backend lines we visit:

The three questions I ask first

  1. What are the package dimensions, down to the thickness? A 1.0 × 0.6 mm part and a 2 × 2 mm part may look close on paper, but they can need completely different input systems.
  2. How does it come in and how must it leave? Tube to tube, tray to tray, or onto tape and reel. The output format usually matters to your customer more than to you.
  3. What does the test need? Number of sites, and whether you test at room temperature only or also hot and cold. A quad-site, tri-temp requirement narrows the field quickly.

With those three answers I can usually tell you which family fits within the same day. Everything after that is speed, contact and price.

Rotary turret handlers

Turret handlers combine multi-site electrical test, 2D/3D vision, laser marking and automated tape and reel in one machine, with index rates up to 35,000 units per hour. They suit high-volume small packages where the output goes straight onto tape.

For very small packages, a turret with bowl-feed input is often the only practical option. Loose parts that size do not travel well through tubes or trays.

Strip test handlers

Strip handlers test devices while they are still on the leadframe or substrate, before singulation. Many devices are contacted at once, which keeps the tester busy and cuts index dead-time. They dock directly to the ATE.

Strip test makes sense when your assembly flow already keeps parts on the strip and you want parallelism without buying a very high-speed singulated handler. If parts are singulated early in your flow, or come from different assembly lines, a turret is usually simpler.

Gravity feed handlers for power discretes

Power packages such as TO-220, TO-247 and D2PAK are fed by gravity through tubes and sorted into multi-bin sort tubes. Contact uses high-voltage Kelvin isolation, rated up to 3,000V dielectric isolation.

Kelvin contact matters here because power devices are measured at milliohm levels. Without separate force and sense contacts, the resistance of the contact itself shows up in your reading, and good parts can fail for on-resistance. The high-voltage isolation keeps the breakdown tests safe for the machine and the operator.

One thing I learned the practical way: a new power package is its own project even when the tester stays the same. We have seen a customer who already runs TO-247 on a line still need a completely separate setup for a newer power package, meaning new sockets, change kits and handler configuration. Plan the budget for that from day one.

KGD sorters and wafer-level burn-in

Bare die and chiplets need precision pick-and-place sorters, often with 6-side micro-crack optical inspection, and sort into gel-pak, waffle pack or tape. Wafer-level burn-in (WLBI) contact equipment is used for advanced chiplet screening.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Assuming a conversion kit will fix a size gap. A common request is to convert an existing handler to a much smaller package. Sometimes it works. Often, for very small parts, the input method itself is wrong, and no kit changes that. Ask before you buy the kit.
  • Choosing the handler before the socket. Contact is where yield is won or lost. Talk to the socket maker early. For new packages we usually sign an NDA with the socket partner first so drawings can be shared openly.
  • Ignoring used equipment. A well-maintained used handler, checked properly, is a sensible way to add capacity or run a qualification without waiting for a new build.
  • Forgetting the brand question. We cannot represent every handler brand. When we cannot supply one, we will tell you straight and suggest an alternative that fits the package, rather than leave you waiting.

Before you ask for a quote

Having these ready saves at least one round of email:

  • Package drawing with dimensions and thickness
  • Input and output format (tube, tray, reel, strip, wafer)
  • Target units per hour and number of test sites
  • Test temperatures (ambient, hot, cold)
  • Tester model the handler will dock to
  • Sample devices, if you can release them
