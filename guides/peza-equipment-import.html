Bringing Test Equipment into a PEZA or Freeport Zone

Who files what, which documents hold shipments up, and what to prepare before the truck arrives. Lessons from our own deliveries into Philippine economic zones.

By John Eliel C. Baladjay, Business Operations Manager · September 25, 2026 · 4 min read

Most of our customers operate inside a PEZA economic zone or a freeport such as Clark. That is good news for costs, because registered enterprises can bring in qualifying capital equipment without the usual duties and taxes. But it adds paperwork, and in my experience the paperwork, not the truck, is what decides when the tester arrives.

This is how the process looks from our side, and what we have learned doing it. It is not legal or customs advice. Your licensed customs broker and your zone administrator have the final word, and the rules do change.

Who does what

Roles in an economic zone equipment shipment
PartyUsual role
Your company (the zone enterprise)Applies for the zone permits, since the registration is in your name. Arranges receiving on the day.
Your customs broker or processorFiles the entry and handles clearance with Customs and the zone authority.
Freight forwarder or courierMoves the shipment and issues the airway bill or bill of lading.
The principal (manufacturer)Issues the commercial invoice, packing list and, where it applies, the certificate of origin.
NXTREVCoordinates all of the above, prepares our own documents, checks everyone's documents against each other, and delivers to your floor.

The background in one paragraph

Under PEZA rules, registered enterprises can import capital equipment, spare parts and accessories free of duties and taxes, as long as they are directly and exclusively used in the registered activity. Local purchases for the registered activity can be VAT zero-rated, and under the CREATE MORE Act the enterprise must secure a VAT zero-rating certificate from PEZA. Freeports such as Clark run under their own authority with their own permits, and the idea is similar. Ask your broker which rules apply to your registration.

Step by step, from purchase order to equipment on the floor

  1. Confirm the exact company name and address. Every document must carry your company name exactly as it appears on your registration or authority to operate in the zone. We once had to reissue the invoice, delivery receipt and agreement because the name was a group name instead of the registered one. Small thing, but the permit office will reject it.
  2. Prepare the commercial documents. Invoice, packing list, delivery receipt, and a loan or sale agreement if the unit is on evaluation. For a loaned unit, the zone authority mainly wants the machine details, its condition, the loan duration and the approval of both parties.
  3. Apply for the permit to bring in. In Clark this is the Permit to Bring-In (PBI), and in our case it was valid for 30 days, so time it close to the delivery date. Your side files it.
  4. Give the truck details. The zone and your security usually need the delivery date, type of truck, plate number and driver's name ahead of time, for the letter of authority or gate pass. We send these as soon as the schedule is fixed.
  5. Deliver and receive. On the day, we call your logistics contact as we enter the zone. Your processor handles the clearance at the gate. Make sure a forklift or mover is ready: the evaluation tester we delivered recently was crated at about 700 × 600 × 1600 mm and up to 100 kg.
  6. Setup. For AccoTEST equipment, the factory engineer comes with the delivery and sets up the tester.

Documents that cause delays

Almost every delay I have seen came from documents that disagree with each other. Before anything ships, we now check:

  • Weight. The gross weight on the invoice must match the weight the courier validates at pickup. A 2 kg difference was enough to hold a shipment of ours until a revised invoice was issued.
  • Unit prices. Each line's unit price must be the line total divided by quantity. An invoice that lists the total in the unit price column will be sent back.
  • Declared value. The value on the airway bill must match the commercial invoice. If they differ, expect a value correction letter before release.
  • Shipper entity. The invoice and the airway bill should be under the same shipping entity.
  • Correction forms. If the courier asks for a correction, use their form exactly. Sending the same information in an email reply creates a second version, and then there are two numbers to reconcile.
  • Certificate of origin. For goods made in China, ask the shipper early whether they can issue a Form E under the ASEAN–China Free Trade Area. It can lower duty on shipments that are not duty-free. It is not always available, so do not hold the shipment for it.

We ask the principal to send the invoice, packing list and airway bill as soon as the shipment is booked, so our broker can prepare the entry before the cargo lands.

Timing

Once the documents are right, delivery itself is quick. What takes time is internal review on the customer's side. A loan agreement going through local counsel and then corporate legal can take several days. Start the agreement and the permit application in parallel, not one after the other.

Prepare before shipment

  • Floor space and a clear path from the loading bay
  • Power and facilities the equipment needs, confirmed with its specs
  • Forklift or mover rated for the crate weight
  • A named logistics contact who can meet the truck
  • Permit dates that cover the planned delivery

Pre-owned equipment and spare boards

Used equipment and spare boards follow the same path, with one addition: describe the condition clearly on every document. "Pre-owned, tested, with diagnostics report" on the invoice avoids questions later. For boards, we attach the diagnostics report to the shipment.
